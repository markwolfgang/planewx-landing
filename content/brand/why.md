origin:
  title: The Origin
  subtitle: How a cross-country flight planning headache became a product.
  paragraphs:
    - >-
      Mark Wolfgang got his pilot's license, bought a plane, and started flying
      a lot. For a trip from Bentonville, Arkansas to Fremont, Nebraska, he
      needed to understand the weather across 500+ miles of airspace. He found
      the Area Forecast Discussion, a detailed NWS narrative covering synoptic
      patterns out to 7 days.
    - >-
      The problem: a single WFO only covers its region. For that flight, Mark
      had to read the **Tulsa AFD**, the **Wichita AFD**, and the **Omaha AFD**,
      three dense, technical documents updated four to six times daily, then
      mentally synthesize a big-picture weather story across the entire route.
  highlightQuote: >-
    I'm flying an SR22 from here to here. Tell me what my likelihood of making
    the trip is.
  highlightLead: 'He copied the AFDs, pasted them into an AI, and asked:'
  highlightAfter: >-
    It worked. The AI could parse the meteorological language and provide useful
    synoptic information. That was the moment PlaneWX was born. The AFD
    synthesis became what is now **Synoptic Intelligence™**.
problems:
  title: The Three Problems
  subtitle: Every feature in PlaneWX traces back to one of these.
  items:
    - icon: AlertTriangle
      accent: amber
      title: 1. The Relevance Problem
      body: >-
        Pull a briefing in your EFB for a flight four days out. It still shows
        you currently available METARs and TAFs, data that's irrelevant to your
        flight timeframe. And it doesn't show you the information that *could*
        be relevant: synoptic patterns, model guidance, the big-picture weather
        story. Inside 24 hours, existing tools are excellent. Beyond that,
        pilots are on their own.
    - icon: Layers
      accent: sky
      title: 2. The Synthesis Problem
      body: >-
        There is no shortage of weather information. aviationweather.gov, your
        EFB's imagery, prog charts. Pilots can cycle through 6-hour, 12-hour,
        24-hour, 2-day, 3-day, 4-day views. But nobody helps you *analyze* it.
        It's up to the pilot, who is not a meteorologist, to piece together
        TAFs, AFDs, prog charts, model data, PIREPs, and SIGMETs into a coherent
        picture of what the weather will look like along their route, at their
        altitude, for their aircraft.
    - icon: Scale
      accent: violet
      title: 3. The Scale Problem
      body: >-
        A weekend pilot with one trip planned can manage the manual process.
        It's tedious, but doable. Now consider a professional pilot who flies
        5–6 trips per week across multiple aircraft. Monitoring weather across
        all those trips, all those routes, all those time frames, manually, is
        simply impossible to do well. PlaneWX gives that pilot an at-a-glance
        dashboard with WX Scores across all active trips.

mentoring:
  title: Why Mentoring Exists
  subtitle: The Steamboat Springs crash and the COPA forum response.
  paragraphs:
    - >-
      When an Epic E1000 crashed in Steamboat Springs, a 5,000-hour pilot in a
      $4 million turboprop, killing everyone on board due to poor
      decision-making, the response on the COPA forum was immediate. Someone
      posted about being a mentor. Pilots started posting their phone numbers.
      The community wanted to be there for each other.
    - >-
      But the process was ad hoc. Texting a friend at 6 AM, hoping they're
      available, hoping they have relevant experience. PlaneWX's mentor system
      formalizes this: experienced volunteers who see the exact same briefing
      data, so the conversation starts with real data, not verbal descriptions
      over the phone.
