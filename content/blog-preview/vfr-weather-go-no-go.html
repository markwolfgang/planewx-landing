A VFR weather go no go call rarely gets difficult at the airport. It gets difficult two or three days earlier, when the bags are half packed, the hotel is booked, and you’re already telling people, “Yeah, we should be there by lunch.” That’s when pressure starts creeping into what should be a clean risk decision.

If you fly real trips in your own airplane, you already know the trap. The weather isn’t bad enough yet to cancel. It isn’t good enough yet to trust. The TAF window may not even reach your departure time. So now you’re trying to decide whether the broad pattern is trending toward a solid VFR day or toward one of those mornings where ceilings sit 800 feet lower than you hoped and the whole plan starts to unravel.

This guide covers how to make that call, and what the FAA’s own data says happens when it goes wrong: VFR into IMC, marginal VFR, scud running, spatial disorientation, and what FAA guidance says to do if you end up in cloud anyway.

Why VFR weather go no go calls are harder than they look

The hard part about VFR isn’t usually legal minimums. It’s margin. A lot of VFR launches are technically legal and still poor decisions for that specific pilot, airplane, route, and mission.

A 3,500 foot broken layer may be comfortable for one pilot in a familiar Skylane on a short daytime hop over flat terrain. That same forecast may be a no-go for another pilot crossing ridges, carrying family, landing near sunset, or counting on a visual arrival into busy airspace. When people talk about weather being “good enough,” they often skip the part that matters most: good enough for whom, where, and with what outs?

That’s why a clean VFR weather go no go framework has to go beyond METAR snapshots. You need the current conditions, of course. But you also need trend, geography, timing, and the larger synoptic setup that explains whether the forecast is holding together or quietly coming apart.

VFR into IMC: what the accident data says

VFR into IMC is what happens when a pilot flying under visual flight rules ends up in instrument meteorological conditions: inside cloud, or in visibility too poor to control the airplane by outside references. The FAA calls this scenario “one of the leading causes of fatal accidents in general aviation.” [1]

Here is what the FAA and the AOPA Air Safety Institute have published about it:

  • 180 accidents, 268 lives. The FAA’s analysis of the NTSB accident database “from 2014 to 2023 reveals 180 IMC-related general aviation accidents, with a fatality rate exceeding 82%.” Those accidents killed 268 people and seriously injured 29 more. [1]
  • Spatial disorientation shows up in about half. “The NTSB cited SD in nearly 50% of accidents they reviewed.” [1]
  • Many were not inadvertent. The FAA found that “more than 40% of accidents show some level of intentional flight into IMC.” [1]
  • A briefing alone did not prevent them. The FAA found that “more than a quarter of accident pilots received a documented briefing and/or demonstrated a clear understanding of the weather conditions they would encounter later.” [1]
  • An instrument rating is not a silver bullet. Across the decade, “about 55%” of the accident pilots lacked an instrument rating, which means a large share held one. The FAA’s conclusion is that instrument training, while it can reduce the risks, “is not a silver bullet.” [1]
  • Nearly always fatal in recent years. In AOPA ASI’s McSpadden Report data for non-commercial fixed-wing flights, VFR into IMC produced 10 accidents in 2023 and all 10 were fatal (14 fatalities). That was 10 of the year’s 15 weather accidents and 10 of its 13 fatal weather accidents. In 2022 it was again 10 accidents, all fatal (16 fatalities), and 10 of the 11 fatal weather accidents. [2]
  • A top 10 cause. The FAA’s 2025 General Aviation Safety Fact Sheet lists “Accidental Flight Into Bad Weather” as number 5 of the top 10 causes of fatal GA accidents from 2014 to 2023. [3]

The two data sets are not directly comparable. The FAA figures cover IMC-related GA accidents over ten years; the AOPA figures cover one weather category for non-commercial fixed-wing flights in a single year. They point the same way, though: this accident type is rare compared with the number of flights, and when it happens it is usually fatal.

Inadvertent IMC vs intentional flight into IMC

Inadvertent IMC sounds like bad luck. Usually it is the end of a chain of small decisions to keep going. The Airplane Flying Handbook gives a practical definition that has nothing to do with the METAR: “Anytime a VFR pilot is unable to maintain airplane attitude control by reference to the natural horizon, the condition is considered to be IMC regardless of the circumstances or the prevailing weather conditions.” [12]

That means a hazy night over dark terrain can be IMC for a VFR pilot even when the reported weather is VFR. It also means the moment you lose the horizon is the moment the situation changed, not the moment you finally admit it.

Marginal VFR: what MVFR means, and what it doesn’t

Marginal VFR, or MVFR, is a weather flight category, not a regulation. The FAA’s Aviation Weather Handbook is explicit: “While the ‘R’ in the acronym means ‘rules,’ there are no 14 CFR part 91 MVFR weather minimums.” [4] Flight categories are the color codes you see on METAR maps. The Handbook defines them this way: [4] VFR needs both values. MVFR, IFR, and LIFR apply when either the ceiling or the visibility falls in the range.

VFR (green)

  • Ceiling: greater than 3,000 ft AGL
  • Visibility: greater than 5 SM

MVFR (blue)

  • Ceiling: 1,000 to 3,000 ft AGL, inclusive
  • Visibility: 3 to 5 SM, inclusive

IFR (red)

  • Ceiling: 500 ft to less than 1,000 ft
  • Visibility: 1 SM to less than 3 SM

LIFR (purple)

  • Ceiling: less than 500 ft
  • Visibility: less than 1 SM

The Handbook adds that the VFR flight category “is not to be confused with the basic VFR weather minimums given in 14 CFR § 91.155” and that “Weather Flight Categories are only intended for situational awareness.” [4]

Can a private pilot fly in marginal VFR?

Legally, yes, as long as the flight meets the basic VFR weather minimums in 14 CFR 91.155 for the airspace you are in. For example, 91.155 requires 3 statute miles of flight visibility and 500 feet below, 1,000 feet above and 2,000 feet horizontal from clouds in Class C, Class D and Class E airspace below 10,000 feet MSL, and it prohibits VFR flight beneath a ceiling of less than 1,000 feet inside surface-area controlled airspace. [5] A ceiling of 1,500 feet and 4 miles is MVFR on the map and can still be legal VFR.

Legal and smart are different questions. The Risk Management Handbook uses exactly this example: “a pilot may legally fly in marginal VFR conditions at night even though low visibility and night hazards increase the risk for an incident or accident.” [6] That gap between legal and smart is what personal minimums are for.

Special VFR is a separate tool. Under 14 CFR 91.157 it requires an ATC clearance, staying clear of clouds and at least 1 statute mile of flight visibility inside surface-area controlled airspace, and for airplanes it is limited to daytime unless the pilot is instrument rated and the airplane is equipped for IFR. [17] It is a way into or out of an airport, not a way to cross a region of low weather.

Scud running: the slow way into IMC

AC 61‑134 puts it plainly: “Operating in marginal VFR/IMC conditions is more commonly known as scud running.” [7] The Pilot’s Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge describes it as what “occurs when a pilot tries to maintain visual contact with the terrain at low altitudes while instrument conditions exist.” [8]

The ending is often not a loss of control in cloud but a collision with the ground. In the AC’s words, “The result is often a CFIT accident when the pilot tries to continue flying or maneuvering beneath a lowering ceiling and hits an obstacle or terrain or impacts water.” [7] The same AC notes the risk is especially great for pilots following a highway under a lowering ceiling, because of the towers along major highways, and it lists as a hazard “Failure to turn around and avoid deteriorating conditions when first able.” [7]

Scud running is a trap because each step feels small. The ceiling comes down 200 feet, then another 200. Visibility goes from 5 to 4 to “about 3.” You descend to stay clear, which puts you closer to towers and terrain and makes it harder to see ahead. Turning back gets harder the further you go, especially in a valley. The AIM’s mountain flying guidance is to avoid “flying into confined areas without adequate room to execute a 180 degree turn, should conditions require. Always fly with a planned escape route in mind.” [9]

The practical defense is to decide your turnaround point on the ground: a ceiling, a visibility, and a terrain condition that mean you turn around, no discussion. If you would not have launched into those conditions, don’t continue into them.

Spatial disorientation and the graveyard spiral

Spatial disorientation is the loss of your correct sense of attitude and motion when you can’t see a horizon. Your inner ear is not built for flight without visual references. The Airplane Flying Handbook explains that “Because of inertia, the sensory areas of the inner ear cannot detect slight changes in airplane attitude, nor can they accurately sense attitude changes that occur at a uniform rate over a period of time.” [12] The AIM adds that “Illusions rank among the most common factors cited as contributing to fatal aircraft accidents.” [10]

Two illusions matter most for VFR pilots who lose the horizon:

  • The leans. After a slow roll that your inner ear never registered, a correction back to level can feel like a bank in the opposite direction. The pilot may lean, or roll the airplane back into the original bank, to “feel” level. [10]
  • The graveyard spiral. The AIM describes it this way: “An observed loss of altitude during a coordinated constant-rate turn that has ceased stimulating the motion sensing system can create the illusion of being in a descent with the wings level. The disoriented pilot will pull back on the controls, tightening the spiral and increasing the loss of altitude.” [10]

Can you recover from a graveyard spiral? Only by rejecting what your body says and flying the instruments: level the wings on the attitude indicator first, then deal with pitch and airspeed. The AIM is blunt that disorientation from these illusions “can be prevented only by visual reference to reliable, fixed points on the ground or to flight instruments.” [10] The FAA’s Fly Safe guidance is to “have confidence in your instruments and ignore all conflicting signals your body gives you.” [11]

Prevention is a planning problem. The same Fly Safe fact sheet says: “Before you fly with less than 3 miles visibility, obtain training and maintain proficiency with flying by instruments.” And: “Do not attempt VFR flight when there is the possibility of getting trapped in deteriorating weather.” [11] The Airplane Flying Handbook adds a sobering number: “Accident statistics show that the pilot who has not been trained in attitude instrument flying, or one whose instrument skills have eroded, lose control of the airplane in about 10 minutes once forced to rely solely on instrument references.” [12]

Caught in IMC: what FAA guidance says to do

This section summarizes published guidance. It is not a substitute for training. The FAA says these procedures “should be demonstrated by and practiced with an instructor. It may be too late to learn them when faced with actual need.” [13]

  1. Accept that it is an emergency. The Airplane Flying Handbook says these situations “should be accepted by the pilot involved as a genuine emergency requiring appropriate action.” [12] The longer you pretend it is temporary, the less time you have.
  2. Fly the airplane by the instruments. The Handbook’s first priority is keeping the wings level, and it says “The pilot needs to believe what the flight instruments show about the airplane’s attitude regardless of what the natural senses tell.” [12] It also says to “Make use of any available aid in attitude control, such as autopilot or wing leveler.” [12]
  3. Make a gentle 180-degree turn back to where the weather was good. The FAA’s guidance is that “If you are not instrument rated and inadvertently encounter instrument weather, the 180 degree turn is usually the best course of action.” [13] The AIM gives the same answer when all visual references are lost: “Trust the cockpit instruments” and “Execute a 180 degree turnaround and start looking for outside references.” [9] The Airplane Flying Handbook says to use the smallest practical bank angle, no more than 10 degrees for a pilot not trained in instrument flying, and that turning a few degrees at a time and then returning to level is acceptable. [12]
  4. Keep everything else gentle. The Handbook says to avoid combined maneuvers such as climbing or descending turns, and that a descent should never exceed 500 feet per minute. [12]
  5. Get help early. AOPA ASI’s escape procedure says: “TALK to ATC immediately and declare an emergency.” [14] Under 14 CFR 91.3, “In an in-flight emergency requiring immediate action, the pilot in command may deviate from any rule of this part to the extent required to meet that emergency.” [16] If the controller’s instructions come too fast, the Handbook says “It’s reasonable to ask the controller to slow down, if this becomes an issue.” [12]

A note on bank angle, because the published sources differ. AOPA ASI’s syllabus says to “EXIT IMC by performing a 180-degree, standard-rate turn until visual references are regained.” [14] The Instrument Flying Handbook’s rule of thumb is that a standard rate turn at 100 knots takes about 15 degrees of bank, [15] which is steeper than the 10-degree limit the Airplane Flying Handbook gives the untrained pilot. [12] We follow the FAA handbook here. Work out with your CFI which technique fits you and your airplane, and practice it under the hood before you need it.

If you are instrument rated and current, the FAA’s advice still starts with getting out: “the goal should remain a rapid escape, not continuation.” The same article adds that “the time to file is before you are in the clouds.” [1]

The best 180 is the one you make before the cloud. AOPA ASI puts it this way: “If conditions worsen after takeoff, diverting early or getting the airplane on the ground before nearing IMC is the safest choice.” [14]

Start with the mission, not the weather

Most bad calls begin with a mission that has already been emotionally approved. After that, every weather data point gets filtered through hope.

Before you look at the first METAR or model run, define what this trip needs to work. Departure time flexibility matters. Arrival flexibility matters. So does the route. If the plan only works if ceilings stay above 4,500 feet for a three-hour leg through rising terrain, with no practical alternates and a family event at the far end, you don’t have a weather question yet. You have a mission fragility question.

This is where PAVE still earns its keep. Not because it’s academic, but because it forces honesty. The pilot may be current and capable. The aircraft may be well equipped. The real problem may be external pressures. If the trip has become important enough that canceling feels expensive, you should assume your judgment is already under load. The FAA’s data backs that up: more than 40% of the IMC accidents it reviewed involved some level of intentional flight into IMC. [1]

Build your VFR go no go around margins

For VFR pilots, margins are the whole game. The issue isn’t whether a forecast squeaks past minimums. The issue is whether it leaves enough room for normal forecast error, normal workload, and normal human bias.

I like to think in layers. First, what ceiling and visibility do I actually want for this route? Second, what terrain and obstacle picture does that create in the real world? Third, what happens if the forecast misses low by 1,000 feet or a few miles of visibility?

That last question matters more than many pilots admit. Forecasts do miss low, especially in marginal regimes involving morning stratus, weak fronts, marine influence, haze, smoke, or convective leftovers. If your plan stops working with a modest miss, it was never a strong plan.

For many cross-country VFR trips, the right no-go decision becomes visible when the route lacks graceful degradation. If there’s no easy turnback, no easy delay, and no easy divert, then you need much better weather than the regs require.

What to look for before the TAF window opens

This is the blind spot that catches a lot of otherwise disciplined pilots. The trip decision often has to be made 48 to 120 hours ahead, long before terminal forecasts are available or stable enough to lean on.

At that stage, I’m not looking for precision. I’m looking for pattern confidence. Is the route under a stable high-pressure setup, or are we watching a slow-moving frontal boundary with wide uncertainty on timing? Are the models generally aligned, or are they arguing about moisture return, low cloud coverage, or convective development? Are local forecast offices flagging concerns in the AFDs that never show up clearly in a simple app view?

This is exactly why broad-area weather context matters so much for a VFR pilot. A single destination forecast can look acceptable while the route in between is setting up for a very different day. AFDs often tell you what forecasters are worried about before those concerns fully express themselves in the usual cockpit planning products.

If I’m watching a trip early, I want to know whether the signal is strengthening or weakening. A deteriorating pattern three days out does not mean automatic no-go. But it does mean I stop making hard commitments like the flight is already happening.

The products matter, but the trend matters more

By launch day, most pilots can assemble the usual stack: METARs, TAFs, radar, satellite, PIREPs, AIRMETs, SIGMETs, winds, and model guidance like HRRR. The question is whether they are reading them as a system or cherry-picking for reassurance.

A route with VFR METARs at both ends can still be a bad VFR launch if the TAF trend is sliding down, the PIREPs show lowering tops and shrinking gaps, and the AIRMET Sierra coverage is expanding into your corridor. The reverse is also true. A marginal early morning picture may be entirely workable if the low stratus burnoff signal is consistent across observations, short-term guidance, and local forecast discussion.

This is where experience helps, but experience has limits. We all have pattern memories that can mislead us. “This usually burns off by 10” is not analysis. It’s a story. Sometimes it’s a good story. Sometimes it’s how pilots end up circling under a lowering deck waiting for the atmosphere to honor last month’s habit.

A practical no-go trigger for VFR pilots

Every pilot should have at least one no-go trigger that is automatic, not negotiated in real time.

For some, it’s ceilings below a route-specific floor. For others, it’s widespread marginal VFR with weak improvement signals. For mountain flying, it may be any setup where cloud bases, visibility, and terrain clearance all tighten at once. For family trips, it may simply be any day where the weather requires too much improvisation to complete on schedule.

That sounds conservative until you’ve lived the alternative. The worst VFR weather decisions are rarely dramatic at engine start. They feel plausible. They feel manageable. They feel like maybe this will work if I just take a look.

Usually, “take a look” is just a way of postponing a decision you already have enough information to make. The same trigger works in the air: write down the ceiling and visibility that mean you turn around, and treat reaching them as the decision already made.

Frequently asked questions

What is VFR into IMC?

VFR into IMC is continued flight under visual flight rules into instrument meteorological conditions, such as cloud or visibility too low to control the airplane by outside references. The FAA’s analysis of NTSB data from 2014 to 2023 found 180 IMC-related general aviation accidents with a fatality rate exceeding 82%. [1]

What is inadvertent IMC?

Inadvertent IMC is an unplanned entry into instrument conditions by a pilot flying VFR. The Airplane Flying Handbook treats any situation where a VFR pilot can no longer control attitude by reference to the natural horizon as IMC, whatever the reported weather, and says it should be accepted as a genuine emergency. [12]

What is the difference between VFR and marginal VFR?

Both are weather flight categories. VFR means a ceiling greater than 3,000 feet and visibility greater than 5 statute miles. Marginal VFR means a ceiling from 1,000 to 3,000 feet and/or visibility from 3 to 5 statute miles. The FAA says there are no Part 91 MVFR weather minimums; the categories are for situational awareness. [4]

Can a private pilot fly in marginal VFR?

Yes, if the flight meets the basic VFR weather minimums in 14 CFR 91.155 for the airspace. MVFR is not a legal limit. The FAA’s Risk Management Handbook notes that marginal VFR at night can be legal while still carrying higher risk, which is why personal minimums matter. [5]⁠[6]

What is scud running?

Scud running is flying low under a lowering ceiling to keep visual contact with the ground while instrument conditions exist. AC 61‑134 says it often ends in a controlled flight into terrain accident when the pilot hits an obstacle, terrain or water. [7]

What should you do if you inadvertently fly into IMC?

Treat it as an emergency, fly by the instruments with wings level, and make a gentle 180-degree turn back toward the good weather. FAA guidance says the 180 degree turn is usually the best course of action for a pilot who is not instrument rated. Use an autopilot if you have one, keep the bank shallow, and talk to ATC and declare an emergency. Practice the procedure with an instructor. [13]⁠[12]⁠[14]

What is a graveyard spiral, and can you recover from one?

A graveyard spiral starts when a pilot in a prolonged coordinated turn loses the sensation of turning, notices the altitude loss, and pulls back on the controls, which tightens the spiral. Recovery means trusting the attitude indicator: level the wings first, then correct pitch and airspeed. [10]

The best VFR calls feel boring

The most disciplined VFR weather go no go decisions usually do not produce heroic stories. They produce uneventful flights, smart delays, and occasional cancellations made early enough that nobody gets bent out of shape.

That’s how it should be. You are not trying to prove that your judgment can rescue a marginal plan. You are trying to build a planning process that keeps weather from cornering you in the first place.

If a trip matters, start early. Watch the trend before the TAFs arrive. Read for deterioration, not just possibility. And if the picture is tightening, make the call while you still have options. That’s not being timid. That’s being PIC.

The confidence to go, or the courage to stay™, starts well before the preflight walkaround.
