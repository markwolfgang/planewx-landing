The hard part of weather judgment usually does not happen on the ramp. It happens three days earlier, when the hotel is booked, your passenger has arranged time off, and you’re staring at a route with no TAFs yet. That is exactly where PAVE risk assessment weather becomes real. Not as a checkbox in a flight review, but as the thing standing between a clean decision and a bad one made under pressure.

Most pilots know PAVE. Pilot, Aircraft, enVironment, External pressures. The trouble is that weather tends to get shoved into the V, then treated like a forecast-review exercise the night before departure. For real trip flying, that is too late. By then the pressure side of PAVE is already fully alive, and it starts bending your judgment whether you admit it or not.

This page covers both FAA checklists pilots use before a flight: IMSAFE, for whether you are fit to fly today, and PAVE, for whether this trip still works. Both are written out in full below, with the weather questions that make them useful on real cross-countries.

What does PAVE stand for in aviation?

PAVE is an FAA risk-management checklist. PlaneWX did not invent it. The letters are a reminder to look at the whole mission before weather, schedule, or get-there-itis narrows your options. The FAA’s Pilot’s Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge (PHAK) presents it as the PAVE checklist, so that “pilots have a simple way to remember each category to examine for risk prior to each flight.” [1]

P: Pilot in command (PIC)

  • Am I current, rested, and willing to fly this weather for this mission?

A: Aircraft

  • Does this airplane’s equipment, performance, and limitations match the forecast picture?

V: enVironment

  • Ceiling, visibility, wind, icing, convection, terrain, night, and airport complexity along the route, not just the destination tab.

E: External pressures

  • Schedule, passengers, cost, pride, and “we already booked it.” Name them early.

Use PAVE as an operational habit, not a form you fill out after the fact. The weather questions below are how that habit earns its keep on real cross-countries.

The IMSAFE checklist: are you fit to fly today?

Before PAVE asks whether the trip works, IMSAFE asks whether you do. The PHAK says: “One of the best ways single pilots can mitigate risk is to use the IMSAFE checklist to determine physical and mental readiness for flying.” [1] IMSAFE stands for Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, and Emotion. Here are the FAA’s questions for each letter:

I: Illness

  • “Am I sick?” [1]
  • The Risk Management Handbook (RMH) goes further: “Does a pilot suffer from initial symptoms, ongoing illness, or any aftereffects?” It adds that “Even mild symptoms may be sufficiently debilitating to create a ‘P’ hazard.” [2]

M: Medication

  • “Am I taking any medicines that might affect my judgment or make me drowsy?” [1]
  • Count over-the-counter drugs too. The RMH notes that “Both prescription and non-prescription over-the-counter (OTC) drugs have side effects that can affect a pilot’s physical and mental performance.” [2]

S: Stress

  • “Am I under psychological pressure from the job? Do I have money, health, or family problems?” [1]
  • The PHAK points out that “While the regulations list medical conditions that require grounding, stress is not among them.” That makes it your call. [1]

A: Alcohol

  • “Have I been drinking within 8 hours? Within 24 hours?” [1]
  • The legal limits are in 14 CFR 91.17. No one may act as a crewmember within 8 hours after drinking any alcoholic beverage, while under the influence, or with an alcohol concentration of 0.04 or greater. [3]
  • Legal is not the same as fit. The RMH warns that “Aftereffects of alcohol consumption (dehydration, hangover, and headaches, etc.) may trigger a ‘P’ hazard for longer than eight hours after consumption.” [2]

F: Fatigue

  • “Am I tired and not adequately rested?” [1]
  • The PHAK calls fatigue “one of the most insidious hazards to flight safety, as it may not be apparent to a pilot until serious errors are made.” [1]

E: Emotion (or Eating)

  • “Am I emotionally upset?” [1]
  • Some versions use the E for Eating. The RMH explains that “some publications combine Emotion with Stress as the ‘S’ in IMSAFE and leave the ‘E’ to remind pilots about ‘Eating.’” On a long flight, plan food and water either way. [2]

Run IMSAFE twice: the night before, when you can still change plans cheaply, and again at the airport. Any honest “yes” on illness, medication, stress, alcohol, fatigue, or emotion is a reason to stop and think about whether the flight should wait, no matter how clean the TAF looks.

PAVE model: how weather leaks into P, A, and External

Weather is not just one quarter of the model. It leaks into every quarter.

A line of low ceilings across your route is obviously an environment problem. But it also becomes a pilot problem if you’re legal and current yet tired, rusty, or not interested in hand-flying an approach to minimums after a long workday. It becomes an aircraft problem if your airplane is technically capable but slow enough that timing around convection gets tight, or if ice protection is limited to a heated pitot and hope. Then it becomes an external pressure problem the minute your family asks, “So are we going or not?”

That is why weather deserves more respect inside the PAVE model than it usually gets. It is rarely isolated. A marginal weather setup amplifies every other weakness in the mission. Pair that thinking with personal minimums for weather planning so the P and V legs stay honest when the forecast starts negotiating.

PAVE risk assessment weather starts before the forecast is precise

Pilots who make consistently good go or no-go calls usually are not better because they found a magic product or read one more METAR. They are better because they start earlier.

Inside 24 hours, you can work with METARs, TAFs, PIREPs, AIRMETs, SIGMETs, and the usual EFB workflow. That part is familiar. The harder problem is 48, 72, or 96 hours out, when the trip is still fluid but the commitments are already becoming real.

At that stage, the question is not, “Will the 1800Z TAF show 900 overcast?” Nobody knows that yet. The real question is, “Is the pattern supporting my mission, or working against it?” That is where many pilots either get complacent or get whipsawed by model noise.

The key is to think in trends and probabilities, not point forecasts. Are the Weather Forecast Offices along your route talking about repeated rounds of convection, a stalled boundary, strengthening onshore flow, or increasing confidence in widespread IFR? Are the model blends moving toward improvement, or just moving the problem six hours left and right? Is the route broadening into a weather system, or narrowing toward a manageable window?

Those are PAVE questions because they shape the decision environment before the decision feels urgent. For how that early window thinking works on small-aircraft missions, see flight weather windows for small aircraft.

How to use PAVE risk assessment weather on a real mission

Let me give you the practical version I wish more pilots used.

Start with the mission, not the weather products. If the trip is a 500 nm family visit in a normally aspirated piston single, your weather tolerance is not the same as a 150 nm solo hop with easy alternates. If you are flying a light twin with deice and solid IFR proficiency, the same synoptic setup may be manageable. PAVE only works when it is specific to the pilot, the airplane, and the actual mission.

Then look at the weather in layers.

First, assess the big pattern. This is where AFDs matter more than most pilots realize. They tell you what the forecaster is worried about, how much confidence they have, and what is driving the changes. If several offices along the route are talking about a persistent marine layer, embedded thunderstorms, or uncertainty around frontal timing, that matters more than one tempting model run showing a clean corridor.

Next, compare that pattern to your actual limits. Not generic personal minimums you wrote in a worksheet five years ago: your current limits. Can you tolerate widespread MVFR with scattered IFR pockets? Night arrival with a gusty crosswind? A route where the freezing level is workable at departure but trends down toward destination? The right answer depends on recency, fatigue, alternates, terrain, and who is in the right seat.

Then deal honestly with pressure. This is the part pilots tend to soften with themselves. If canceling the trip means disappointing clients, missing a family event, or eating hotel costs, external pressure is high whether or not you feel emotional about it. Naming that pressure early matters because pressure gets stronger as departure gets closer. It never gets weaker.

Finally, keep revising. Good PAVE use is not a one-time score. It is a moving decision process. Weather changes, but so does the rest of PAVE. Maybe the route improves while your fatigue worsens. Maybe the convective picture stabilizes but your return leg starts looking trapped. The mission can become a no-go even when the departure looks fine.

PAVE checklist for a real cross-country

Use this as a trip-planning scan, not a student worksheet dump. It combines the FAA’s PAVE questions from the PHAK with weather-first prompts for real trips. Adjust the thresholds to your airplane, your currency, and this mission. [1]

Pilot

  • IMSAFE done honestly, the night before and again at the airport?
  • The PHAK’s core question: “Am I ready for this trip?” in terms of experience, recency, currency, physical, and emotional condition. [1]
  • Current and proficient for the approaches, night, or mountain work this route may require?
  • Fatigue, illness, medication, and recent IFR (or lack of it) honest for the forecast workload?
  • Personal minimums written down before this trip, not adjusted for it?

Aircraft

  • “Is this the right aircraft for the flight?” [1]
  • Familiar with and current in this airplane? The PHAK reminds pilots that book performance figures come from a new aircraft flown by a professional test pilot. [1]
  • Equipment and performance match the weather (ice protection, oxygen, climb, fuel, avionics for the expected IMC)? Instruments, lights, navigation and communication equipment adequate?
  • Runways available with an adequate margin for the conditions? Planned load within limits? Able to operate at the altitudes the trip needs?
  • Fuel capacity with reserves for every planned leg, and fuel delivered matching fuel ordered? [1]
  • Known squawks or limitations that become deal-breakers in this pattern?

enVironment

  • Pattern supporting the mission 48 to 96 hours out (AFDs, NBM-style probability), not just a pretty model corridor?
  • Route chain workable: departure, climb, pinch points, fuel stops, arrival, alternate (not destination-only)?
  • Icing, convection, wind, ceiling and visibility, terrain, and night inside your current personal minimums?
  • Thunderstorms present or forecast? If there are clouds, any icing, current or forecast? Temperature and dew point spread? Can a descent be made safely all along the route? [1]
  • Crosswind component at each airport, and strong winds aloft if the route crosses mountains? [1]
  • Terrain and obstacles: safe altitudes chosen in advance from the VFR and IFR charts, especially for night or low visibility? [1]
  • Airports and airspace: lighting at destination and alternates, NOTAMs for closed runways or lights out, TFRs along the route? [1]
  • A plan if the forecast is wrong? The PHAK’s advice: “Consider the possibility that the weather may be different than forecast. Have alternative plans and be ready and willing to divert, should an unexpected change occur.” [1]

External pressures

  • Hotel, passengers, meetings, and “we already told them we’re coming” named out loud?
  • The PHAK’s list of pressures, checked honestly: someone waiting at the airport, a passenger you don’t want to disappoint, the desire to demonstrate your qualifications or impress someone, get-there-itis, and pride. [1]
  • A pre-agreed decision checkpoint before packing and ramp pressure take over?
  • Refundable or movable options still open if the trend worsens?
  • Personal SOPs in place, as the PHAK suggests: time for an extra fuel stop or an unexpected weather landing, backup airline reservations for must-be-there trips, and for really important trips, leaving early enough that you could still drive. [1]

The last letter deserves the most attention. The PHAK says “Management of external pressure is the single most important key to risk management because it is the one risk factor category that can cause a pilot to ignore all the other risk factors.” [1]

You remain PIC. A checklist helps you see the picture. It does not make the go/no-go call.

PAVE vs IMSAFE

Pilots often hear both acronyms in the same safety talk and treat them as interchangeable. They are not.

IMSAFE is primarily about you as the pilot: Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Emotion (or Eating, depending on the version you learned). [2] It is a fitness-to-fly check. If you fail IMSAFE, you should not be launching, regardless of how clean the TAF looks.

PAVE is the whole mission. It still includes the pilot, but it also forces Aircraft, enVironment (where weather lives), and External pressures into the same frame. A rested, sober, current pilot can still have a high-risk PAVE picture if the airplane is under-equipped for icing, the route is boxed in by convection, and the family vacation is already paid for.

Use IMSAFE first when you are asking whether you should be flying at all. Use PAVE when you are asking whether this trip still works. In practice IMSAFE becomes the heart of the P. Weather risk assessment lives mostly in the V, but it only becomes honest when P, A, and E are in the same conversation.

What pilots get wrong about weather and external pressure

The most common mistake is treating weather as a final-answer problem. Pilots wait for certainty, then force a late decision from incomplete information. That tends to create two bad outcomes. You either launch because the latest forecast looks just good enough, or you cancel after everyone is already packed and frustrated.

A better approach is to reduce surprise earlier.

That means watching whether uncertainty itself is increasing. If the timing of a front keeps slipping, if the ceiling forecast keeps bouncing between workable and ugly, or if convective wording in the AFDs keeps getting stronger, that is information. Uncertainty is not a gap in the briefing. It is part of the risk picture.

Another mistake is separating route weather from destination weather too neatly. For serious cross-country flying, the route is often the real story. You may have VFR at both ends and still face widespread buildups, mountain obscuration, low freezing levels, or no comfortable outs in the middle. PAVE helps because it forces you to connect the dots operationally, not just scan airport tabs.

You can practice this without any pressure at all. The FAA suggests planning flights you never intend to fly: “These fictional flights are a great way for pilots to run through their FRATs and decision-making process. Since there isn’t any intent to fly, external pressures are eliminated while skill is built.” [5]

Frequently asked questions

What does PAVE stand for in aviation?

PAVE stands for Pilot in command, Aircraft, enVironment, and External pressures. It is the FAA’s checklist for dividing the risks of a flight into four categories so each one gets examined before departure. [1]

What is the IMSAFE checklist?

IMSAFE is the FAA’s personal fitness-to-fly checklist: Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, and Emotion. The PHAK describes it as one of the best ways single pilots can mitigate risk by checking their physical and mental readiness for flying. Some versions use the final E for Eating. [1]⁠[2]

What is the difference between IMSAFE and PAVE?

IMSAFE checks the pilot only: are you fit to fly today? PAVE checks the whole flight: the pilot, the aircraft, the environment including weather, and external pressures. IMSAFE is best treated as the core of the P in PAVE.

What are the alcohol rules for pilots?

Under 14 CFR 91.17, no one may act as a crewmember within 8 hours after drinking alcohol, while under the influence, or with an alcohol concentration of 0.04 or greater. The FAA’s Risk Management Handbook notes that aftereffects can create a hazard for longer than eight hours. [3]⁠[2]

Is PAVE only for flight reviews?

No. Flight reviews and insurance seminars are where many pilots first hear the letters. The useful place to use PAVE is days before a real trip, while hotels, passengers, and work schedules are still movable. If you only open the checklist the morning of departure, the External pressures leg has already done most of its damage. Weather risk assessment inside PAVE starts when the pattern is still a trend, not when the TAF finally exists.

What is a FRAT, and how does it relate to PAVE?

A flight risk assessment tool (FRAT) turns the PAVE questions into a scored form. The FAASTeam’s own FRAT follows the PAVE checklist. The FAA stresses that “A FRAT is not meant to make your go/no-go decision for you.” [4]

From PAVE worksheet to a living FRAT

PAVE is the thinking model. A FRAT is how that model stays alive as the flight evolves. The FAA’s Fly Safe guidance makes the case for writing it down: “Putting everything on ‘paper’ allows us to establish our risk limits in an atmosphere free from the pressure of an impending flight.” [4] Its Cleared for Takeoff blog adds that “using a flight risk assessment tool (FRAT) is a good way to enforce standards, such as personal minimums.” [5]

The goal is not perfection

No serious pilot expects weather to become easy. Forecasts move. Patterns evolve. The atmosphere does not care about your schedule.

What you can do is make the decision earlier, with better context and less self-deception. That is what PAVE is supposed to do at its best. Not fill out a matrix after the fact, but help you see when weather is starting to infect the whole mission.

If the trip is shaping up well, you want the confidence to go. If it is not, you want the courage to stay™. Give yourself enough lead time to know the difference. When you want that picture earlier, open PlaneWX and walk the mission against your limits while you still have options.
