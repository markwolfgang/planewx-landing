Decision guidance is from the Airplane Flying Handbook, FAA‑H‑8083‑3C, the Risk Management Handbook, FAA‑H‑8083‑2A, and 14 CFR Part 91. Sources are listed at the end.

On a VFR cross-country, waiting for weather to pass is usually the lower-risk choice when the weather sits between you and your destination and its timing is uncertain. Leaving early works only when the whole trip, including the arrival and a fuel reserve, fits inside a forecast window with margin to spare, in daylight, and with an out you have already chosen. The FAA lists waiting for the weather to improve and delaying the flight as ways to reduce risk, and its handbook examples for both a warm front and a cold front end the same way: stay on the ground until the front has passed. [3]⁠[2] The hard part is not the rule. It is reading what kind of weather you are dealing with, and being honest about why you want to go now.

This post is about that same-day decision. For planning days ahead and finding a usable window in the first place, see weather windows for small aircraft.

The short answer: when to leave early and when to wait

Leaving early can make sense when

  • The hazard is forecast to arrive well after your planned landing, at the destination and along the route
  • The weather is the kind that builds with daytime heating, such as afternoon showers, and you can land before it develops [1]
  • The TAF timing, the GFA route picture and the latest observations agree with each other [1]
  • You land in daylight with your fuel reserve intact, and you have a named alternate behind you, away from the weather [6]⁠[3]

Waiting is usually better when

  • A front or line of storms lies across the route, and leaving early means racing it [2]
  • Morning fog or low stratus is forecast to lift, and leaving early means departing into it [1]
  • The only way the trip works is if the forecast timing is exactly right
  • The main reason to go now is a schedule, a passenger or a plan you do not want to change [2]⁠[4]

Sometimes neither works. A slow front or a stationary front can hold an area for days, and the right call is another day or another way to travel. [1]⁠[2]

What each choice is betting on

Leaving early and waiting are both bets on timing, and they fail in different ways.

  • Leaving early bets that the weather will arrive no sooner than forecast, and that you will be on the ground before it does. If the system moves faster, you meet it en route, often near the destination, when fuel, daylight and options are lowest.
  • Waiting bets that conditions after the weather are acceptable for you, and that the delay does not create new risks such as night, fatigue, gusty winds, or a stronger urge to go once you finally can.

A useful test is to ask which failure you would rather manage. A pilot who waited and was wrong is usually on the ground, irritated, with options. A pilot who left early and was wrong may be in the air, low on choices, under weather the FAA handbooks say not to fly in.

Read the timing, not just the flight category

What the TAF can and cannot tell you

A TAF covers the expected conditions “within 5 SM of the center of the airport’s runway complex (terminal).” Most TAFs cover 24 hours, and some major airports get 30. [1] It says nothing about the 100 miles in between, which is where a leave-early plan usually goes wrong.

  • FM (from): used when prevailing conditions “are expected to change significantly over a period of less than one hour.” An FM group is the forecaster’s best timing for a change, not a guarantee. [1]
  • TEMPO: temporary fluctuations with a greater than 50 percent probability, lasting an hour or less each time, and covering less than half of the period in total. If the TEMPO conditions are below your minimums, plan as if they will happen while you are there. [1]
  • PROB30: a 30 percent chance of a thunderstorm or precipitation event. Low probability is not no probability, especially at the destination, where you have the least room. [1]
  • TAF AMD: TAFs are amended when they become “unrepresentative of existing or expected conditions,” and an amended TAF supersedes the previous one. An amendment that moves a change earlier is strong evidence against leaving early. [1]

Use the GFA for the route

The Graphical Forecasts for Aviation (GFA) Tool is meant to “give users a complete picture of the weather that may impact their flight operations,” including clouds, flight category, precipitation, thunderstorms and wind. [1] It also carries a warning that matters for this decision. Users can switch overlays off, and “This can lead to hidden areas of hazardous weather for a given flightpath.” [1] Before you decide to leave early, check the GFA with AIRMET and SIGMET overlays on, for the full route, at the hour you expect to be over each segment.

Check the trend against the forecast

Compare the latest METARs along the route with what the TAFs said those hours would look like. If observations are running ahead of the forecast, the weather is probably moving faster than planned, and leaving early is a race you may lose. The PHAK’s advice on briefings applies here: “It is always a good idea for the pilot to update the weather information whenever he/she has additional time.” [2] An early outlook is not enough on its own: “A prudent pilot requests a follow-up briefing prior to departure since an outlook briefing generally only contains information based on weather trends and existing weather in geographical areas at or near the departure airport.” [2]

What kind of weather are you waiting for?

The right answer changes with the weather. These are the common cases the FAA handbooks describe.

A cold front ahead

Cold fronts move at “a rate of 25 to 30 mph,” and extreme cold fronts have been recorded moving at up to 60 mph. [1] They often bring “a narrow band of showers and thunderstorms along, or just ahead of, the front,” and a squall line may form along or ahead of it. “Squall lines present a serious hazard to pilots as squall-type thunderstorms are intense and move quickly.” [1]

The PHAK’s sample flight from Pittsburgh toward St. Louis meets a cold front near Indianapolis, with thunderstorms and heavy rain showers. Its conclusion: “A pilot using sound judgment based on the knowledge of frontal conditions will likely remain in Indianapolis until the front has passed.” It adds, “Trying to fly below a line of thunderstorms or a squall line is hazardous, and flight over the top of or around the storm is not an option.” [2]

  • Leaving early: means racing a line that can move faster than forecast. It only works if you land well before the front with margin at the destination.
  • Waiting: often works well, because “Behind a fast-moving cold front, the skies usually clear rapidly, and the front leaves behind gusty, turbulent winds and colder temperatures.” [1] Check those gusts and crosswinds against your personal minimums before you go.
  • The exception: if the warm air ahead of the front was stable, “overcast skies and rain may occur for some distance behind the front.” [1] Waiting a few hours may not be enough.

A warm front ahead

“Warm fronts move slowly, typically 10 to 25 mph,” and they favor “widespread layered or stratiform cloudiness and precipitation along, and ahead of, the front.” After passage, “The visibility eventually improves, but hazy conditions may exist for a short period after passage.” [1]

In the PHAK’s warm front example, the flight leaves Pittsburgh in good VFR, but beyond Indianapolis the ceiling and visibility are too low to continue. Its advice: “it would be wise to remain in Indianapolis until the warm front passes, which may take up to two days.” [2] Leaving early ahead of a warm front often means flying into lowering ceilings, not ahead of them. Waiting may mean a different day.

Morning fog and low stratus

This is the case where leaving early is most often the wrong idea. Radiation fog “is generally a nighttime occurrence and often does not dissipate until after sunrise.” “Ground fog usually burns off rather rapidly after sunrise. Other radiation fog generally clears before noon unless clouds move in over the fog.” [1] Low stratus behaves the same way, tending to be lowest during night and early morning and lifting by late morning or early afternoon. When rain soaks the ground and the sky clears, radiation fog is not uncommon the next morning. [1]

For a dawn departure, check the destination and alternates, not just your home field. An early arrival can land you at a field still in fog that your departure airport has already shed.

Afternoon showers and thunderstorms

Scattered convection forms “usually during the afternoon in response to diurnal heating,” and those air mass storms “almost always dissipate near or just after sunset.” [1] Here leaving early is often the better plan, because a morning flight lands before the build-up starts. Waiting for afternoon storms to end usually means arriving at dusk or after dark, which brings its own rules.

A stationary front

A stationary front “remains stationary and influences the local weather for days.” [1] Neither leaving early nor waiting a few hours fixes that. Look at another day, another route, or another way to travel.

The constraints that decide it: daylight, fuel and you

Daylight

Waiting has a cost when it pushes your arrival toward night. The Airplane Flying Handbook is direct: “Pilots should avoid a VFR night flight if expecting conditions below VFR minimums.” It adds, “Even for experienced pilots, night VFR operations should only be conducted in unrestricted visibility, favorable winds, both on the surface and aloft, and no turbulence.” And “Generally, at night, it is difficult to see clouds and restrictions to visibility, particularly on dark nights or under an overcast.” [3] If waiting means landing after dark in post-frontal gusts or lingering haze, the wait has not bought you a better flight. It may mean staying overnight.

Fuel

Under 14 CFR 91.151, you need enough fuel, “considering wind and forecast weather conditions,” to reach your first point of intended landing and then fly at least 30 minutes by day or 45 minutes at night. [6] A later departure can change both halves of that: different winds behind a front, and the night reserve if you land after dark. Leaving early to beat weather has a fuel trap too. A diversion around building weather near the destination can use the reserve you planned to keep.

You

A long wait at the airport is tiring, and fatigue is one of the conditions the Risk Management Handbook says calls for higher minimums, adding at least 500 ft to your ceiling. [4] Our guide to personal minimums weather planning covers those adjustments in detail.

Get-there-itis is the hidden variable

The weather question is often the easy part. The PHAK describes get-there-itis as a disposition that “impairs pilot judgment through a fixation on the original goal or destination, combined with a disregard for any alternative course of action.” [2] It sits next to two pitfalls that a bad leave-early decision can lead to:

  • Scud running: “This occurs when a pilot tries to maintain visual contact with the terrain at low altitudes while instrument conditions exist.” [2]
  • Continuing VFR into instrument conditions: “Spatial disorientation or collision with ground/obstacles may occur when a pilot continues VFR into instrument conditions.” [2]

The Risk Management Handbook names the source: “The pressure to get to a particular destination or to leave at the conclusion of a planned stay can affect a pilot’s judgment.” And “Pilots subject to external pressures sometimes fail to consider the other three major categories of hazards.” [4] Its mitigations are practical. Tell the people waiting for you that the trip needs flexibility, and plan alternative travel options to reduce the pressure. [4]

A simple check: if the weather were the same but nobody was waiting for you, would you still leave early? If not, the decision is being made by the schedule.

Build your out before you choose

An out is the plan you will use if the weather is not what you expected. The regulations and handbooks agree that you need one before you start. Under 14 CFR 91.103, preflight action for a flight away from the airport vicinity must include “weather reports and forecasts, fuel requirements, alternatives available if the planned flight cannot be completed.” [5] The Airplane Flying Handbook says: “A pilot should always have viable alternatives available in the event the original flight plan cannot be accomplished.” [3]

The PHAK gives a ground-level example. With a meeting in Washington and low IFR forecast at the destination, a pilot “might consider reserving a rental car at an airport in northern North Carolina or southern Virginia to coincide with a refueling stop.” That way “the pilot has an out to avoid being pressured into continuing the flight if the conditions do not improve.” [2] The Risk Management Handbook adds why it works: “If the pilot prepares for scenarios that may occur during a flight, such as a diversion or precautionary landing, it becomes easier to consider and perform that option with the needed information at hand.” [4]

  • Alternates on the good side: at least one airport behind you, away from the weather, with fuel and a way home
  • A turnaround point: a place or time on the route where you will land or turn back if conditions are worse than forecast, decided on the ground
  • Ground options: a rental car, a hotel or a later airline seat at the stop where you might divert
  • A precautionary landing mindset: the Airplane Flying Handbook lists deteriorating weather as a reason for a precautionary landing, and warns that the non-instrument-rated pilot trapped by weather who does not consider one “accepts an extremely hazardous alternative.” [3]

A side-by-side check before you decide

If you are thinking about leaving early

  • Does the whole route and the arrival fit inside the forecast window with margin, not just the departure?
  • Do METARs, TAFs and the GFA agree, with no TAF amendment moving the change earlier?
  • Is the hazard behind you at the start, or ahead of you at the end?
  • Do you have a named alternate away from the weather and the fuel to reach it?

If you are thinking about waiting

  • What do conditions look like after the weather: gusts, crosswind, haze, lingering low clouds?
  • Will you still land in daylight, with your legal and personal fuel reserve?
  • Will you be rested enough to fly to your normal minimums?
  • If the wait runs long, what is your plan for tomorrow?

If the answers point to neither, the FAA’s list of ways to reduce risk ends with options pilots forget are on it: delay the flight, cancel the flight, or drive. [3] The Airplane Flying Handbook says that when marginal weather creates a risk of inadvertent IMC for a pilot not ready for it, “Proper risk mitigation would require that flight be canceled or delayed until weather conditions were not conducive for inadvertent flight into instrument meteorological conditions.” [3]

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to leave early or wait for weather on a VFR cross-country?

It depends on the weather type, but waiting is usually the lower-risk choice when the weather lies across your route and its timing is uncertain. FAA handbook examples for both warm and cold fronts advise staying on the ground until the front passes. Leaving early can work for afternoon convection, when you can land before it builds, with daylight, fuel and an alternate to spare. [2]⁠[1]

How long does it take for a front to pass?

It varies. The Aviation Weather Handbook gives typical speeds of 25 to 30 mph for cold fronts and 10 to 25 mph for warm fronts. In the PHAK’s warm front example, waiting for the front to pass may take up to two days. A stationary front can influence the local weather for days. [1]⁠[2]

Is the weather good after a cold front passes?

Often, but not always. Behind a fast-moving cold front the skies usually clear rapidly, but the front leaves gusty, turbulent winds. If the warm air ahead was stable, overcast skies and rain may continue for some distance behind it. Check winds and crosswind against your minimums. [1]

Should I leave at dawn to beat the weather?

Not if the morning has fog or low stratus. Radiation fog often lasts until after sunrise, and other radiation fog generally clears before noon unless clouds move in. Check the destination and alternates, not just your departure field. A dawn departure makes most sense ahead of afternoon convection, not into morning fog. [1]

What does PROB30 or TEMPO in a TAF mean for my timing?

TEMPO means temporary changes with a greater than 50 percent probability, each lasting an hour or less. PROB30 means a 30 percent chance of a thunderstorm or precipitation event. If either is below your minimums at your arrival time, plan for it. [1]

What counts as an out on a VFR cross-country?

An alternate away from the weather with fuel to reach it, a turnaround point decided on the ground, and a way to finish the trip without flying, such as a rental car at a fuel stop. 14 CFR 91.103 requires you to know the alternatives available if the planned flight cannot be completed. [5]⁠[2]
