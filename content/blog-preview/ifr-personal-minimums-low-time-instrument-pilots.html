Last reviewed: October 1, 2026. Legal requirements are from 14 CFR Parts 61 and 91 (eCFR). The method follows the FAA Personal Minimums Checklist, FAA‑P‑8740‑56, and the Risk Management Handbook, FAA‑H‑8083‑2A. Sources are listed at the end.

A newly instrument-rated pilot should set IFR personal minimums well above the legal ones, starting from the lowest conditions they have recently and regularly flown with comfort, not from the numbers on the approach chart. The FAA does not publish one set of IFR personal minimums. It gives you a method: separate numbers for precision and non-precision approaches, a limit on missed approaches, your own takeoff minimums, wind, ice and thunderstorm rules, and a recency requirement tighter than legal currency. [1]⁠[2] Then lower them slowly, one value at a time, after real experience and training, and never while planning a specific flight. [1]⁠[2]

This is the instrument companion to our guide to personal minimums weather planning, which covers the VFR side and the full FAA checklist. Here the focus is the first few hundred hours after the checkride, when the rating says you may and your logbook is still deciding whether you should.

The short answer: IFR personal minimums for a new instrument pilot

  • Ceiling and visibility: the lowest IFR conditions you have recently and regularly flown with comfort, with separate day and night values [2]
  • Approach type: one ceiling and visibility above published minimums for precision approaches, and a separate pair for non-precision approaches [1]
  • Missed approaches: how many you will fly before you divert, decided in advance [1]
  • Takeoff: your own takeoff ceiling and visibility, because Part 91 sets none for most private IFR flights [1]⁠[8]
  • Alternate and fuel: an alternate with better weather than the legal minimum, and more fuel than the legal reserve [1]⁠[6]⁠[7]
  • Wind: the strongest surface wind, gusts and crosswind you have comfortably handled in the last six to twelve months [2]
  • Ice and thunderstorms: yes-or-no rules you can check before departure [2]⁠[3]⁠[10]
  • Currency: legal currency as the floor, with a personal recency requirement above it [1]⁠[5]

Legal IFR minimums vs personal IFR minimums

The regulations say when an IFR flight is allowed. They do not say when it is wise for you. The Risk Management Handbook puts it plainly: “Federal regulations that apply to aviation do not cover every situation nor do they guarantee safety.” [2] The Instrument Flying Handbook is just as direct about the rating: “Holding the instrument rating does not necessarily make you a competent all-weather pilot.” It adds, “Your instrument rating permits you to fly into instrument weather conditions with no previous instrument weather experience.” [3]

That second sentence is the case for personal minimums. The checkride does not give you hours of actual IMC, night approaches to minimums, or a gusty crosswind at the bottom of a non-precision approach. “Your instrument rating is issued on the assumption that you have the good judgment to avoid situations beyond your capabilities.” [3]

The legal baseline for a Part 91 IFR flight

  • Instrument currency, 61.57(c): within the 6 calendar months before the month of the flight, six instrument approaches, holding procedures and tasks, and intercepting and tracking courses, in actual conditions or with a view-limiting device. A full flight simulator, flight training device or aviation training device can count. [5]
  • Lapsed currency, 61.57(d): after more than six calendar months out of currency, only an instrument proficiency check restores it. [5]
  • Alternate required, 91.169(b): unless the destination has an instrument approach and, from 1 hour before to 1 hour after your ETA, the forecast is at least a 2,000 ft ceiling and 3 statute miles visibility. [6]
  • Alternate weather, 91.169(c): the published alternate minimums, or if none, 600 ft and 2 statute miles for a precision approach and 800 ft and 2 statute miles for a non-precision approach. [6]
  • Fuel, 91.167: enough to reach the destination, then the alternate if one is required, then 45 minutes at normal cruising speed. [7]
  • Takeoff, 91.175(f): the civil takeoff minimums apply to operations under Parts 121, 125, 129 and 135, not to most private Part 91 flights. [8]

The Instrument Flying Handbook closes its own discussion of recency with five words worth keeping: “Remember, these are minimum requirements.” [3]

Why low time changes the numbers

Right after the rating, the gap between what is legal and what you have actually done is at its widest. The Risk Management Handbook builds that into its method: “If flying experience is limited or infrequent, the adjustment magnitude could increase.” [2] It also separates currency from proficiency. Its example pilot is legally IFR current, “However, if these currency events occurred five months ago, the pilot may lack the proficiency to make the flight safely.” [2] AC 61‑134 makes the same point about accidents: “IFR operations can be dangerous for those not prepared to operate or not current and proficient in the IMC and IFR environments.” [9]

Low time also means fewer reference points. A new instrument pilot may have flown an approach to minimums only under a view-limiting device, in smooth air, with an instructor aboard, while an experienced pilot has flown it in rain, gusts and fatigue. Same chart, different workload.

How to set each IFR personal minimum

The FAA Personal Minimums Checklist gives the structure. Its IFR weather section asks for ceiling and visibility above minimums for precision and non-precision approaches, a maximum number of missed approaches before diverting, and takeoff minimums. Its Pilot section asks how many instrument approaches and instrument flight hours, simulated or actual, you have flown recently. And it tells you to “Give yourself permission to choose higher minimums than those specified in the regulations, aircraft flight manuals, or other rules.” [1]

Ceiling and visibility

Record what you have actually flown, not what you are allowed to fly. “For IFR, Figure 2-4 shows how a pilot recorded the lowest IFR conditions recently and regularly experienced.” The Handbook then adds a caution that fits every new instrument pilot: “Although a pilot may have successfully flown in low IFR (LIFR) conditions, it does not mean the pilot was ‘comfortable’ in these conditions.” [2]

  • Where to start: the lowest ceiling and visibility you have flown recently and regularly in actual conditions without feeling behind the airplane
  • Day and night: separate values; the FAA sample pilot uses a higher night number than day number [2]
  • How to write it: as feet and miles above published minimums, as the checklist does, or as fixed values, as the Handbook sample does [1]⁠[2]

Approach type

The checklist splits precision from non-precision approaches for a reason. [1] AC 61‑134 lists “The increased CFIT risk of nonprecision approaches” among its IFR risk factors. [9] The Instrument Procedures Handbook notes that “A constant-rate descent has many safety advantages over non-precision approaches that require multiple level-offs at stepdown fixes or manually calculating rates of descent.” [4]

GPS approaches need a decision of their own. “Approaches with vertical guidance (APV) are non-precision approaches because they do not meet the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) Annex 10 standards for a precision approach.” [4] So LPV and LNAV/VNAV sit with non-precision approaches by definition, even though a glidepath may fly much like an ILS. Decide which column your vertically guided approaches use, write it down, and use it every time.

  • Precision (ILS): ______ ft and ______ miles above published minimums
  • Vertically guided (LPV, LNAV/VNAV): your chosen column, written down
  • Non-precision (LNAV, VOR, LOC): ______ ft and ______ miles above published minimums, normally higher than your precision numbers
  • Circling: a separate decision; the Instrument Procedures Handbook says “Circling approaches are one of the most challenging flight maneuvers conducted in the NAS” [4]
  • Missed approaches: no more than ______ before diverting [1]

Two planning rules follow. First, the approach you plan may not be the approach you get: “the wrong combination of wind and visibility can make all instrument approaches at an airport unavailable.” And “Pilots must be prepared to execute other available approaches, not just the one that they may have planned for.” [4] Set minimums for the approach you would actually be left with. Second, define a stable approach before you fly one. AC 61‑134 describes a PIC who “is prepared to make a missed approach when conditions fall below minimums as specified in the regulations, company policy, pilot’s personal minimums checklist, or the approach becomes destabilized,” and recommends a minimum stabilized height of 1,000 ft above the airport or touchdown zone elevation in IMC. [9]

Takeoff minimums

Because Part 91 gives most private IFR flights no takeoff minimums, the checklist asks for your own. [1]⁠[8] Ask what you will do if something goes wrong right after takeoff. If the answer is to return and land, your takeoff minimum must leave you an approach you can fly back. If it is a takeoff alternate, name it before engine start.

Alternates and fuel

The alternate rules in 91.169 are the floor. [6] Pick an alternate with clearly better weather, ideally outside the same weather system, so a missed approach leads somewhere easier. AC 61‑134 explains why: “many GA aircraft flown IFR have limited range or speed to fly out of unforecasted weather conditions.” [9] The checklist has its own line for IFR fuel reserves, day and night, in hours. [1] Fill it in above the legal 45 minutes. [7]

Wind and turbulence

The Risk Management Handbook says to “record the most challenging wind conditions comfortably experienced during the last six to twelve months.” [2] The Handbook’s sample single-engine values are 10 knots of surface wind, 5 knots of gusts and a 7 knot crosswind component. They are one sample pilot’s numbers, not a recommendation. [2]

Ice

For most new instrument pilots in airplanes without known-icing approval, ice is a yes-or-no rule. The Instrument Flying Handbook’s warning is short: “Structural icing is a condition that can only get worse.” [3] The Risk Management Handbook gives the shape of a usable rule: “Many instrument pilots who operate aircraft that are not approved for flight in known icing conditions apply personal minimums to avoid conditions below certain temperatures or where the freezing level is at or below the minimum en-route altitude.” [2] The checklist keeps it to one line: icing conditions within aircraft and pilot capabilities. [1]

Thunderstorms and embedded convection

The FAA handbooks write these as rules, not numbers to tune. “Do not take off in the face of an approaching thunderstorm or fly an aircraft that is not equipped with thunderstorm detection in clouds or at night in areas of suspected thunderstorm activity.” [3] The Aviation Weather Handbook adds: “Do not fly without airborne radar into a cloud mass containing scattered embedded thunderstorms.” It also advises avoiding any thunderstorm identified as severe, or giving an intense, heavy or extreme radar echo, by at least 20 miles. [10]

Currency and proficiency

This is where low-time pilots most need a number above the legal one. Six approaches in six months is legal. [5] The Handbook’s case study shows why legal is not the same as ready: “She is IFR current, but it has been four months since her last proficiency event.” To compensate, she adds 500 ft to her personal minimums for the instrument approach at her destination. [2]

  • Approaches: ______ in the last ______ days, some in actual conditions [1]
  • Instrument flight hours: ______ in the last ______ days [1]
  • Hours in this make and model: ______ in the last ______ days [1]
  • If you fall short: raise ceiling and visibility, fly with an instructor, or practice in a training device first

An example worksheet, built the FAA way

This shows the method, not recommended values. Every number below is an FAA sample or example adjustment. Your starting point comes from your logbook.

Step 1: the FAA sample IFR baseline (Risk Management Handbook, Figure 2-4) [2]

  • Day: 800 ft ceiling, 1 mile visibility
  • Night: 999 ft ceiling, 3 miles visibility
  • LIFR: left blank, because the sample pilot decided to avoid it

Step 2: the FAA example adjustments (Figure 2-9) [2]

  • Pilot (illness, medication, stress, fatigue, or lack of currency): add at least 500 ft to ceiling
  • Aircraft (unfamiliar airplane, avionics or equipment): add at least 1/2 mile to visibility
  • Environment (unfamiliar airport, airspace or terrain): add at least 500 ft to runway length
  • External pressures (must-meet deadlines, passengers): subtract at least 5 knots from winds

“If multiple special conditions apply, a pilot could make an adjustment for each factor.” For a night cross-country to an unfamiliar airport after a full workday, the Handbook raises the baseline by 1,000 ft of ceiling, one mile of visibility and 1,000 ft of runway length. [2]

Step 3: what that does to the sample night minimum

  • FAA sample night baseline: 999 ft and 3 miles
  • With the Handbook’s night cross-country adjustment: 1,999 ft and 4 miles
  • Your version: ______ ft and ______ miles

That is the point. Apply the FAA adjustments honestly for fatigue, an unfamiliar field and night, and a new instrument pilot will often need weather far better than the chart minimums. With limited or infrequent experience, the Handbook says the adjustments could be larger. [2]

How to step IFR minimums down as experience grows

Personal minimums should move. The checklist says, “Periodically review and revise your checklist as your personal circumstances change, such as your proficiency, recency, or training.” It also sets the direction: “You should never make your minimums less restrictive unless a significant positive event has occurred.” And: “However, it is okay to make your minimums more restrictive at any time.” [1]

The Risk Management Handbook adds three cautions: [2]

  1. Not for a specific flight. “The time to consider adjustments is not while under pressure to fly, but rather when time and objectivity permits an honest self-analysis about skill, performance, and comfort level during the last few flights.”
  2. One value at a time. “Make adjustments to one variable at a time.”
  3. With help. “Seek training and consult with a flight instructor before making a significant adjustment to personal minimums.”

The checklist does not define a significant positive event. In our reading, good candidates change your evidence, not just your confidence: an instrument proficiency check or recurrent training, approaches in actual conditions with an instructor, transition training in a new airplane or avionics, or a run of flights at your current numbers that went well without feeling rushed. Lower one value a little after such an event and leave the rest alone until it feels routine. A long layoff or a new airplane is a reason to move numbers back up.

Use them on the day

Written minimums only help if they decide something. From the Instrument Flying Handbook: “If weather minimums are below a pilot’s personal minimums, a delay in departure to wait for improved conditions is a good decision.” [3] The checklist adds a rule for the whole picture, “If you have marginal items in two or more risk factors/categories, don’t go!” and a warning: “Never make your minimums less restrictive when you are planning a specific flight, or else external pressures will influence you.” [1]

The Handbook’s last step is to stick to the plan: “Professional pilots live by the numbers, and so should general aviation pilots.” [2] The decision stays where 14 CFR 91.3 puts it: “The pilot in command of an aircraft is directly responsible for, and is the final authority as to, the operation of that aircraft.” [11] For reading the trend before an IFR departure, see a weather workflow for instrument pilots and what weather trends matter for IFR departures.

Minimums on a card get negotiated on the ramp. Minimums every briefing is scored against are harder to argue with.

Here is how your IFR numbers carry through the loop:

  • Your minimums, saved once. In personal minimums you set a Comfort (soft) and a Max (hard) limit for icing, turbulence, crosswind, ceiling, visibility and storm avoidance distance. Airport-specific minimums hold a separate ceiling, visibility and crosswind for up to 25 airports, such as a higher ceiling where the only approach is non-precision. Aircraft limits apply when they are more restrictive.
  • A WX Score against those numbers. Frequently asked questions

What IFR personal minimums should a new instrument pilot use?

There is no single FAA set. Start from the lowest IFR conditions you have recently and regularly flown with comfort, then add margin for night, fatigue, an unfamiliar airport or a long layoff. As one example, the Risk Management Handbook’s sample pilot uses 800 ft and 1 mile by day and 999 ft and 3 miles at night. [1]⁠[2]

Is being IFR current the same as being proficient?

No. Currency under 14 CFR 61.57(c) is six approaches, holding, and intercepting and tracking courses within the 6 calendar months before the month of the flight. The Risk Management Handbook notes that a current pilot may still lack proficiency if those events were months ago, and its example pilot adds 500 ft to her approach minimums for that reason. [5]⁠[2]

Should I use different minimums for an ILS and an RNAV (GPS) approach?

Many pilots do. The FAA checklist has separate lines for precision and non-precision approaches, and the Instrument Procedures Handbook classifies approaches with vertical guidance, such as LPV and LNAV/VNAV, as non-precision. Decide which column your vertically guided approaches use and keep it consistent. [1]⁠[4]

When can I lower my IFR personal minimums?

Only after a significant positive event, and never while planning a specific flight. Change one variable at a time, and get training or talk to a flight instructor before a significant change. Making minimums more restrictive is fine at any time. [1]⁠[2]

What are the legal IFR alternate requirements?

Under 14 CFR 91.169 you need an alternate unless the destination has an instrument approach and, from 1 hour before to 1 hour after your ETA, the forecast is at least a 2,000 ft ceiling and 3 statute miles. The alternate must forecast its published alternate minimums, or 600 ft and 2 statute miles for a precision approach and 800 ft and 2 statute miles for a non-precision approach. [6]

Should a low-time instrument pilot fly in icing or near thunderstorms?

Not in an airplane without known-icing approval, and not into clouds with embedded thunderstorms without detection equipment. The Instrument Flying Handbook warns that structural icing can only get worse. Write these as yes-or-no rules, not numbers to stretch. [3]⁠[10]
