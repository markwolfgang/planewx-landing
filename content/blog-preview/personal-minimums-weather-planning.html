You usually do not bust your personal minimums weather planning at the runway. You bust it three days earlier, when you tell your spouse, your customer, or your hotel that you are definitely flying.

That is when the pressure starts. The forecast still looks fuzzy. There is no useful TAF for your ETA. Maybe the big-picture pattern looks manageable, maybe not. But once the trip feels committed, your weather standards have a way of getting softer without you ever saying it out loud. That is why weather minimums are not just numbers for departure day. They are planning tools for the days before the flight, when external pressure is still small enough to manage.

This guide puts the legal VFR and IFR weather minimums next to the personal minimums the FAA encourages you to set, walks through the FAA’s own Personal Minimums Checklist, and then covers how to use those numbers days before a trip.

Why personal minimums weather planning often fails early

Most pilots think of personal minimums as a preflight checklist item. Ceiling, visibility, crosswind, convective risk, icing, maybe an approach requirement for the destination and alternate. That matters, but it is only half the job.

The harder part is deciding when the weather picture is uncertain enough that you should stop making promises. A route with marginal MVFR ceilings in the TAF window is one thing. A trip four days out with a slow-moving trough, shaky model agreement, and AFD language hinting at timing uncertainty is something else. If your minimums only exist at wheels-up time, you are missing the point where they could save you the most stress.

That is where a lot of otherwise disciplined pilots get trapped. They are current, proficient, and honest about their limits. But they use those limits too late. By the time the METARs and TAFs sharpen up, the mission already has momentum.

Legal VFR and IFR minimums vs personal minimums

The regulations set a floor, not a target. The FAA’s Risk Management Handbook opens its personal minimums chapter this way: “Federal regulations that apply to aviation do not cover every situation nor do they guarantee safety.” [9] The Pilot’s Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge calls “the difference between what is ‘legal’ in terms of the regulations, and what is ‘smart’ or ‘safe’ in terms of pilot experience and proficiency” one of the most important concepts safe pilots understand. [10]

Here are the legal numbers, split into short lists so each one reads on a phone. Your personal minimums sit on top of them.

Basic VFR weather minimums (14 CFR 91.155)

Flight visibility and distance from clouds by airspace, from the table in 91.155(a): [3]

Class A

  • Not applicable

Class B

  • Visibility: 3 statute miles
  • Clouds: clear of clouds

Class C, Class D, and Class E below 10,000 ft MSL

  • Visibility: 3 statute miles
  • Clouds: 500 ft below, 1,000 ft above, 2,000 ft horizontal

Class E at or above 10,000 ft MSL

  • Visibility: 5 statute miles
  • Clouds: 1,000 ft below, 1,000 ft above, 1 statute mile horizontal

Class G, 1,200 ft AGL or less (airplanes)

  • Day: 1 statute mile, clear of clouds
  • Night: 3 statute miles; 500 ft below, 1,000 ft above, 2,000 ft horizontal
  • Night exception, 91.155(b)(2): with at least 1 statute mile, an airplane may operate clear of clouds in the traffic pattern within 1/2 mile of the runway

Class G, above 1,200 ft AGL and below 10,000 ft MSL

  • Day: 1 statute mile; 500 ft below, 1,000 ft above, 2,000 ft horizontal
  • Night: 3 statute miles; 500 ft below, 1,000 ft above, 2,000 ft horizontal

Class G, above 1,200 ft AGL and at or above 10,000 ft MSL

  • Visibility: 5 statute miles
  • Clouds: 1,000 ft below, 1,000 ft above, 1 statute mile horizontal

Other legal VFR limits

  • Ceiling in surface areas, 91.155(c): no VFR flight beneath a ceiling of less than 1,000 ft within the lateral boundaries of controlled airspace designated to the surface for an airport. [3]
  • Ground visibility, 91.155(d): to take off, land, or enter the traffic pattern under VFR within a Class B, C, D, or E surface area, ground visibility must be at least 3 statute miles (or flight visibility, if ground visibility is not reported). [3]
  • Special VFR, 91.157: with an ATC clearance, clear of clouds, and at least 1 statute mile flight visibility, below 10,000 ft MSL in surface-area controlled airspace. For airplanes, only between sunrise and sunset unless the pilot meets the instrument requirements and the aircraft is IFR-equipped. [4]
  • VFR fuel, 91.151: enough to reach the first point of intended landing plus at least 30 minutes during the day, or 45 minutes at night, at normal cruising speed. [5]

How do you remember the VFR minimums? Most of the table reduces to one pattern: 3 statute miles, and 500 below, 1,000 above, 2,000 horizontal. That pattern covers Class C, Class D, Class E below 10,000 ft MSL, and Class G at night. One way to hold on to it is “3-152.” Then learn the exceptions: Class B is clear of clouds, above 10,000 ft MSL it becomes 5 miles and 1,000/1,000/1 mile, and low-level Class G by day is 1 mile and clear of clouds.

One more distinction: MVFR is not a legal minimum. The FAA’s Aviation Weather Handbook says flight categories “are only intended for situational awareness” and that “there are no 14 CFR part 91 MVFR weather minimums.” [11]

Legal IFR weather limits (Part 91)

  • Alternate required? 91.169(b): you may omit an alternate only if the destination has an instrument approach and, for at least 1 hour before to 1 hour after your ETA, the ceiling is forecast to be at least 2,000 ft above airport elevation and visibility at least 3 statute miles. [7]
  • Alternate minimums, 91.169(c): the alternate minimums published for the procedure, or if none, 600 ft and 2 statute miles for a precision approach, or 800 ft and 2 statute miles for a nonprecision approach. With no instrument approach, the alternate must allow descent from the MEA, approach, and landing under basic VFR. [7]
  • Below DA/DH or MDA, 91.175(c): only if the aircraft is continuously in position for a normal descent to the intended runway, the flight visibility is not less than the approach requires, and the required visual references are distinctly visible and identifiable. [8]
  • IFR fuel, 91.167: enough to reach the destination, then the alternate if one is required, then 45 minutes at normal cruising speed. [6]
  • Takeoff: the civil takeoff minimums in 91.175(f) apply to operations under Parts 121, 125, 129, and 135. Part 91 sets no standard IFR takeoff minimums for other flights, which is exactly why the FAA checklist asks you to set your own. [8]⁠[1]

Legal minimum vs a sample personal minimum

The FAA’s Risk Management Handbook walks through a sample pilot’s personal minimums. They are one pilot’s numbers, not a recommendation for you, but they show how far above the legal floor a thoughtful pilot might sit. [9]

Day VFR ceiling and visibility

  • Legal: cloud clearance and visibility for the airspace (above), and a 1,000 ft ceiling in surface areas
  • FAA sample personal minimum (MVFR, day): 2,500 ft ceiling, 4 miles visibility
  • Your personal minimum: ______ ft, ______ miles

Night VFR ceiling and visibility

  • Legal: mostly 3 statute miles and 500 ft below, 1,000 ft above, 2,000 ft horizontal
  • FAA sample personal minimum: 5,000 ft ceiling, 8 miles visibility
  • Your personal minimum: ______ ft, ______ miles

IFR ceiling and visibility

  • Legal: the minimums on the approach chart you fly, and 91.175(c) to go below them
  • FAA sample personal minimum: 800 ft and 1 mile by day; 999 ft and 3 miles at night
  • Your personal minimum: ______ ft, ______ miles, or ______ ft and ______ miles above published minimums

Wind (single-engine)

  • Legal: Part 91 sets no wind limit; know your POH’s demonstrated crosswind
  • FAA sample personal minimum: 10 knots surface wind, 5 knots gusts, 7 knots crosswind component
  • Your personal minimum: ______ kt wind, ______ kt gusts, ______ kt crosswind

Fuel reserve

  • Legal: 30 minutes day VFR, 45 minutes night VFR, 45 minutes IFR (after the alternate, if required)
  • Your personal minimum: ______ hours VFR day, ______ night, ______ IFR

The FAA Personal Minimums Checklist

The FAA already publishes a personal minimums checklist, and it is the best starting point. It is FAA‑P‑8740‑56, from the FAA Safety Team, and the same checklist appears as Appendix D of the Aviation Instructor’s Handbook. [1]⁠[2] It is organized by PAVE, and every line is either a blank for your number or an item to think about.

The FAA’s instructions are short. “Give yourself permission to choose higher minimums than those specified in the regulations, aircraft flight manuals, or other rules.” “Use it at home as you start planning a flight and again just before you make your final decision to fly.” It calls this practicing “Conservatism Without Guilt.” [1]

Here is the checklist, section by section, as the FAA lays it out: [1]

Pilot

  • Experience and recency: takeoffs and landings, hours in make and model, instrument approaches (simulated or actual), and instrument flight hours, each “in the last ___ days”; terrain and airspace familiar
  • Physical condition: sleep in the last 24 hours; food and water in the last ___ hours; no alcohol, drugs or medication in the last ___ hours; no stressful events or illnesses in the last ___ days

Aircraft

  • Fuel reserves (cross-country): VFR day and night, IFR day and night, in hours
  • Experience in type: takeoffs and landings in the aircraft type in the last ___ days
  • Aircraft performance: additional performance available over that required; consider gross weight, load distribution, density altitude, and performance charts
  • Aircraft equipment: familiar with the avionics (including autopilot and GPS), COM/NAV appropriate to the flight, current charts, clothing suitable for preflight and flight, survival gear appropriate for the flight and terrain

Environment

  • Airport conditions: crosswind as a percentage of the POH maximum; runway length as a percentage more than the POH requirement
  • Weather: reports and forecasts not more than ___ hours old; icing conditions within aircraft and pilot capabilities
  • Weather for VFR: ceiling day and night (feet); visibility day and night (miles)
  • Weather for IFR: for precision and non-precision approaches, ceiling in feet and visibility in miles above minimums; no more than ___ missed approaches before diverting; takeoff minimums ceiling and visibility

External pressures

  • Trip planning: allowance for delays in minutes; diversion or cancellation alternate plans; notification of the people you are meeting; passengers briefed on diversion or cancellation plans; modifying or canceling car, restaurant, or hotel reservations; alternative transportation arranged
  • Personal equipment: credit card and telephone numbers for alternate plans; clothing and personal needs, such as eyewear and medication, for an unexpected stay
  • Importance of trip: “The more important the trip, the more tendency there is to compromise your personal minimums, and the more important it becomes to have alternate plans.”

Two rules from the checklist are worth copying onto the first page of your logbook. First: “If you have marginal items in two or more risk factors/categories, don’t go!” Second: “Never make your minimums less restrictive when you are planning a specific flight, or else external pressures will influence you.” [1]

How the FAA says to build your numbers

The Risk Management Handbook lays out six steps for setting personal minimums: [9]

  1. Review the weather flight categories. Know the ceiling and visibility ranges for VFR, MVFR, IFR, and LIFR.
  2. Assess your experience and comfort level. Fill in a certification, training, and experience summary, then record the lowest conditions you have recently and regularly flown with comfort.
  3. Consider other conditions. Add wind, gusts, crosswind, and turbulence limits, plus performance factors such as shortest runway, highest terrain, and highest density altitude.
  4. Assemble and evaluate. Combine everything into a baseline personal minimums table.
  5. Adjust for specific conditions. Raise the baseline when the flight adds risk.
  6. Stick to the plan. In the Handbook’s words, “Professional pilots live by the numbers, and so should general aviation pilots.”

For step 5, the Handbook’s example adjustments are a starting point you can tailor: [9]

  • Add at least 500 ft to your ceiling minimum
  • Add at least 1/2 mile to your visibility minimum
  • Add at least 500 ft to your required runway length
  • Subtract at least 5 knots from your wind limits

It applies them when the pilot is facing illness, medication, stress, fatigue, or lack of currency; an unfamiliar airplane or avionics; unfamiliar airports, airspace, or terrain; or must-meet deadlines and passenger pressure. For a night cross-country to an unfamiliar airport after a full workday, its example raises the baseline by 1,000 ft of ceiling, one mile of visibility, and 1,000 ft of runway. [9]

The Handbook also gives three cautions: don’t adjust your personal minimums to complete a specific flight; change one variable at a time; and get training and consult a flight instructor before any significant adjustment. [9]

IFR personal minimums

What are good IFR personal minimums? The FAA does not publish one set of numbers, because the right answer depends on your recency, your airplane, and the approach. What it gives you is structure. The Personal Minimums Checklist asks you to set ceiling and visibility above published minimums for precision and non-precision approaches, a maximum number of missed approaches before you divert, and your own takeoff minimums. [1] The Risk Management Handbook’s sample instrument pilot uses 800 ft and 1 mile by day and 999 ft and 3 miles at night. [9]

A few questions to answer when you set yours:

  • How many approaches have you flown to minimums, in actual conditions, in the last few months?
  • Is the autopilot reliable enough that you would want to rely on it at the end of a long day?
  • Would you accept an alternate that is itself close to minimums, or do you want one with VFR or near-VFR weather?
  • Do you need higher numbers in mountainous or unfamiliar terrain? The PHAK suggests it: “In mountainous terrain, consider having higher minimums for ceiling and visibility, particularly if the terrain is unfamiliar.” [10]
  • What is your takeoff minimum, given that Part 91 does not set one for you?

Set weather minimums for the planning phase, not just launch time

A useful set of personal minimums should include two layers. The first is your operational layer: the conditions you are willing to depart, continue, and arrive in. The second is your planning layer: the conditions under which you are willing to commit to the trip before the short-range forecast exists.

Those two layers should not be identical. In fact, the planning layer is often more conservative because uncertainty itself is a risk factor. A 1,500-foot ceiling at the destination might be acceptable to an instrument-rated pilot with a good alternate, solid fuel, and a current approach setup. But three or four days out, when the only thing you really know is that multiple forecast offices are discussing widespread low clouds and timing disagreement, that same trip might not be commit-worthy yet.

This is one place where PAVE becomes practical instead of theoretical. The “V” (enVironment) is not just the weather at takeoff. It is the weather pattern, the forecast confidence, and the way that uncertainty interacts with the rest of the trip. The “P” is not just your rating. It is your real recent experience flying in that kind of system. The “A” includes whether your aircraft can handle the route, altitude, ice exposure, and winds. The “E” (External pressures) includes the pressure you create when you announce the trip too early.

Personal minimums weather planning should account for trend

Pilots love a single number because it feels objective. Ceiling 800, visibility 2, crosswind 15. Clean. But weather planning for real trips is rarely about a single number.

Trend matters more than snapshot conditions when you are looking beyond 24 hours. If an AFD is talking about improving conditions behind a front, lifting ceilings, and decreasing precip coverage, that may fit your planning minimums even if the exact timing is still a little uncertain. If the discussion points to deteriorating conditions, embedded convection, lowering snow levels, or strengthening winds near your arrival window, that should tighten your standards even if one model run still looks friendly.

This is also where route matters. A destination with decent alternates, lower terrain, and multiple outs is different from a route that funnels you between weather systems, terrain, or sparse fuel options. A pilot flying a turbocharged single with FIKI is making a different call than a pilot in a normally aspirated piston single trying to stay out of ice entirely. Same map. Different minimums.

Good personal minimums weather planning is specific enough to reflect that reality. Not just “no icing.” More like, “No planned flight in forecast icing beyond a short climb or descent window, and no dispatch if the freezing level and cloud tops leave me without an exit.” Not just “avoid thunderstorms.” More like, “No commitment to a summer afternoon arrival if convective timing is uncertain and there is no reliable morning option.”

Use the products that explain why the forecast is moving

If you are only checking a string of future app forecasts or model snapshots, you can miss the bigger setup. The better question is not just what one model says for your ETA. It is why the forecast is changing.

AFDs are useful because they show forecaster thinking. They expose uncertainty, model disagreement, timing concerns, and local effects that do not jump out of a graphic. NBM probabilities add discipline because they give you a range instead of a single deterministic answer. Closer in, HRRR can help with timing details, but it should not be mistaken for certainty just because the picture looks precise.

By departure day, you are back in the familiar scan: METAR, TAF, PIREP, SIGMET, AIRMET, radar, and the rest of your normal workflow. But for personal minimums weather planning, the value is earlier. You want enough lead time to see whether the pattern is stabilizing or getting worse before the trip becomes emotionally expensive.

That is the gap many pilots feel on business trips and family travel. The weather tools they use every day become much more useful inside 24 hours. The hard commitment decisions usually happen before that.

Build your minimums around decision points

One thing that helps is to stop treating go or no-go as a single moment. Real trips have several decision points, and your weather standards can match them.

At four or five days out, your question is whether to hold the trip loosely, book refundable plans, or start warning people that the flight may not happen. At two or three days, you are asking whether the pattern still fits your planning minimums. Inside 24 hours, you move to operational minimums and alternate strategy. The closer you get, the more tactical your choices become.

That sounds obvious, but many pilots skip the middle stage. They go from vague optimism to departure-day pressure with nothing in between. If you instead say, “I need a stable trend by 48 hours or I am switching to Plan B,” your personal minimums become a decision tool, not a statement on a clipboard.

Keep your minimums honest

Personal minimums drift. Usually downward, and usually for reasons that have nothing to do with proficiency.

Sometimes the drift is subtle. You flew a similar trip last month, so this one feels routine. Sometimes it is emotional. The family is packed, the meeting matters, or the airplane has to be home by Monday. Sometimes it comes from familiarity with a route, which can be helpful, but can also make you too casual about a pattern that is objectively unstable.

The fix is not to be fearful. It is to be explicit. Write down not just your launch minimums, but your commit minimums. Define what forecast uncertainty you will accept 72 hours out, 48 hours out, and 24 hours out. Revisit those standards after trips that felt easy and after trips that felt tight. If you diverted, delayed, or canceled, ask whether the problem was the weather or the point at which you let the mission harden. The FAA’s checklist puts the rule simply: you “should never make your minimums less restrictive unless a significant positive event has occurred. However, it is okay to make your minimums more restrictive at any time.” [1]

A good set of minimums does not need to be complicated. It does need to reflect who you are as a pilot right now, what your airplane can actually do, and how much uncertainty you are willing to carry before external pressure starts making decisions for you.

Frequently asked questions

What are the basic VFR weather minimums?

They are in 14 CFR 91.155. In Class C, Class D, and Class E airspace below 10,000 ft MSL, you need 3 statute miles of flight visibility and must stay 500 ft below, 1,000 ft above, and 2,000 ft horizontally from clouds. Class B requires 3 statute miles and clear of clouds. At or above 10,000 ft MSL in Class E, it is 5 statute miles and 1,000 ft below, 1,000 ft above, and 1 statute mile horizontal. Class G by day at or below 1,200 ft AGL requires 1 statute mile and clear of clouds for airplanes. [3]

What weather do you need to fly VFR?

Legally, the flight visibility and cloud clearance in 91.155 for your airspace, plus a ceiling of at least 1,000 ft and ground visibility of at least 3 statute miles to operate under VFR in a Class B, C, D, or E surface area. Special VFR under 91.157 is the exception, with an ATC clearance. Most pilots set personal minimums well above those numbers. [3]⁠[4]

How do you remember VFR minimums?

Learn the common pattern first: 3 statute miles, and 500 ft below, 1,000 ft above, 2,000 ft horizontal from clouds, which covers Class C, Class D, Class E below 10,000 ft MSL, and Class G at night. Then learn the exceptions: Class B is clear of clouds, at or above 10,000 ft MSL it is 5 miles and 1,000/1,000/1 mile, and low Class G by day is 1 mile and clear of clouds. [3]

What is a personal minimums checklist?

It is a written set of your own limits, set above the legal minimums, for the pilot, aircraft, environment, and external pressures. The FAA publishes one, the Personal Minimums Checklist (FAA‑P‑8740‑56), which is also Appendix D of the Aviation Instructor’s Handbook. The FAA says to use it at home as you start planning a flight and again just before your final decision to fly. [1]⁠[2]

What are good IFR personal minimums?

There is no single right set. The FAA checklist asks you to set ceiling and visibility above published approach minimums, a limit on missed approaches before diverting, and your own takeoff minimums. The FAA Risk Management Handbook’s sample instrument pilot uses 800 ft and 1 mile by day and 999 ft and 3 miles at night. [1]⁠[9]

Is marginal VFR a legal minimum?

No. MVFR is a weather flight category (ceiling 1,000 to 3,000 ft and/or visibility 3 to 5 statute miles). The FAA says there are no Part 91 MVFR weather minimums; the categories are for situational awareness. [11]
